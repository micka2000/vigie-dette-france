How it is calculated

FranceCanada
FR

The Canadian Vigie Index sums up the state of Canadian federal debt in a score from 0 to 100. Here is how it is calculated, with the example of 8 November 2026.

← Back to the dashboard · Benchmarks reviewed on 8 November 2026

In short

Four signals are tracked. Each gets a score out of 100 from a scale: Canadian history for the gap and cover, France’s scale for non-residents and ratings. The two “markets” signals are averaged, then the two “fundamentals” signals. The index is the average of these two parts. It is the same calculation as for France.

Index = ( Markets + Fundamentals ) ÷ 2

Markets      = ( Gap + Cover ) ÷ 2
Fundamentals = ( Non-residents + Ratings ) ÷ 2

Index = ¼·Gap + ¼·Cover + ¼·Non-residents + ¼·Ratings

Each signal counts for a quarter of the index. The Canadian index is read against Canada’s own history: a Canadian 40 does not compare with a French 40.

There is no Canadian equivalent of TARGET2: Canada has its own currency. A fifth signal, pressure on the Canadian dollar, is under study and is not part of the index.

2. Each signal becomes a score out of 100

Each signal has five benchmarks: the score is 0 at the first, 25 at the second (watch), 50 at the third (stress), 75 at the fourth (crisis) and 100 at the last. Between two benchmarks, the score moves in a straight line, as for France. The benchmarks come from two rules.

  • Gap and cover: the percentiles of their history. The score is 0 at the 25th percentile (a better level was seen only one time in four), 25 at the median, 50 at the 75th percentile, 75 at the 90th and 100 at the 99th (a worse level was seen only one time in a hundred). For the gap, the history starts in 1982, in monthly averages (Statistics Canada, Federal Reserve): it includes Canada’s 1994-1995 debt crisis, when the gap was 150 to 200 bp. For cover, it starts in 2001.
  • Non-residents and ratings: France’s scale. The agencies’ scale is the same for every country. The risk of debt held abroad, sellers who can leave all at once, depends on the share held, not on the country. In Canada, this share has mostly followed demand for debt seen as safe: 33% during the 1995 crisis, 14% in 2009, 47% in 2026. Percentiles would have put it in “crisis” at its record.

For cover, a low value is bad: the scale goes down. Over the 2001-2026 sessions, the gap was in “calm” 73% of the time, in “watch” 24%, in “stress” 4% and never in “crisis”; the non-resident share never reached 50%. These benchmarks are fixed: they do not move with new data.

3. The two parts and the index

The “markets” part is the average of the gap and cover; the “fundamentals” part, that of non-residents and ratings. A part is calculated only when both its signals are up to date, never with one alone: a signal that is too old makes its part, and therefore the index, unavailable.

SignalKept for up toWhy
Gap7 daysa long holiday weekend in Canada or the United States, plus a margin
Cover5 monthsat most 127 days between two 10-year auctions since 1998
Non-residents4 monthspublished about two and a half months after month end
Ratingsno limita rating holds as long as it has not changed

With these durations, the index could have been calculated at every one of the 6,339 sessions since 2 January 2001.

5. What is not in the index

The Bank of Canada’s financial stress index, published monthly, is shown next to the index for comparison: it measures strain across all Canadian markets (equities, currency, credit), not only on federal debt. It is converted to 100 by the percentile rule, over 2001-2026: 0.03; 0.06; 0.13; 0.20; 0.47. Its record, 0.55, dates from February 2009.

6. Known limits

  • The gap with the United States is not a pure risk premium. The two countries share neither a currency nor a central bank: the gap also follows their policy rates. Its high, +272 bp in April 1990, and its record since 2001, +134 bp on 9 October 2002, came mostly from monetary policy, not a debt crisis. It is kept because it is the only free daily market measure.
  • A high non-resident share can also reflect confidence. This is why it follows France’s scale rather than Canadian history: its 2026 record (46.9% in July) stays “calm”, below the 50% threshold.
  • Non-resident numerator and denominator, checked on 8 November 2026. Both count bonds only: no Treasury bills, no Crown corporations, no retail savings products. They differ on two points. Bonds held abroad are at book value (accrued interest included), the total at nominal value. And they include foreign-currency bonds, 2.8% of foreign holdings in August 2026 (7.8% at the end of 2015): without them, the share would be 44.6% instead of 45.9%. Before 1995, the series also counted repurchase agreements: it therefore starts in January 1995.
  • The Canadian index moves little. Over 2001-2026, it never went above 31 (on 18 December 2008, with its “markets” part at 61) and was in “watch” in only 4% of sessions. It does not go back to the 1995 crisis: 10-year bond auctions are only published from 1998.
  • The benchmarks were reviewed on 8 November 2026. The first version drew all four signals except ratings from 2001-2026 percentiles: the gap entered “crisis” from 43 bp, far from the 1995 crisis, and the non-resident record put the index in watch (27 on 8 November 2026, against 5.8 now).
