The Vigie Index over time

Show the descriptionHide the description

What it is. A score from 0 to 100 that sums up the state of French debt, month by month.

Why it matters. To compare today with past crises: 2008, the euro crisis in 2011, Covid in 2020. When the index rises while the rest of the markets stay quiet, French debt itself is the worry.

How to read the chart. In blue, the Vigie Index. In green, its “markets” part: what investors demand to lend to France. In dotted grey, stress across all financial markets in France, used as a benchmark. Each month shows its most strained day. Coloured backgrounds give the level; the eye in the legend hides or shows a line.

Who measures it, and when. Vigie Dette France, from the five signals on this page (calculation method, formula, data); market stress comes from the European Central Bank. Since May 2008, recalculated every morning using only the information known on each day.

    France-Germany spread

    155 bpCrisis

    10-year OAT 4.93% · Bund 3.38%

    Show the descriptionHide the description

    What it is. The gap between the rate at which France borrows over 10 years and that of Germany, seen as the safest country in the euro area.

    Why it matters. The wider the gap, the riskier investors judge lending to France. Since 2004, it has stayed below 50 points 7 days out of 10; its record, 214 points, dates from 17 November 2011.

    How to read the chart. The gap is given in basis points: 100 points equal 1%. At 150, France pays 1.5% more than Germany every year to borrow. In Spread view, coloured backgrounds show the risk thresholds; the Yields button shows the two rates themselves, in %, without thresholds.

    Who measures it, and when. The Banque de France for the French rate and the Bundesbank for the German rate, every day at market close since 2004; collected every morning.

        Agence France Trésor auctions

        2.00Watch
        Show the descriptionHide the description

        What it is. Investor demand when the State auctions its 10-year bonds to borrow.

        Why it matters. Weakening demand means the State is finding it harder to attract lenders, and will have to pay them more. Below 2, demand is getting thin; below 1.5, it is clearly weakening.

        How to read the chart. Each point is an auction: amount bid divided by amount sold. At 2, buyers wanted twice as much as was sold. Coloured backgrounds show the risk thresholds; the rate paid at each auction is in the auction details.

        Who measures it, and when. The Agence France Trésor, which sells on behalf of the State, usually on the first Thursday of the month, since 2004; its results are reported by Reuters (latest auction) and collected every morning. August and December auctions, which are special, are set aside.

          Share held by non-residents

          59.7%Stress
          Show the descriptionHide the description

          What it is. The share of the State’s debt held by foreign investors.

          Why it matters. The higher this share, the more France depends on capital that can leave quickly in a crisis. Above 60%, it would be back to the dependence of 2008 to 2016.

          How to read the chart. In blue, State debt, the one that counts in the index. In green, all public debt (State, local authorities, social security): it is lower because part of it is lent by French banks. Coloured backgrounds show the risk thresholds.

          Who measures it, and when. The Banque de France for State debt, every quarter since 2008; Eurostat for all public debt, once a year.

              Credit ratings

              A+Stress
              Show the descriptionHide the description

              What it is. The ratings that judge France’s ability to repay its debt. AAA is the best rating.

              Why it matters. A downgrade makes borrowing more expensive for France. Because review dates are known in advance, markets await each one nervously. Two steps really matter. Below A-, banks get less money from the European Central Bank when they pledge French debt as collateral (ECB rule). Below BBB-, French debt would drop out of the “investment grade” category: many funds, which are not allowed to hold it, would have to sell, and the ECB would no longer accept it.

              How to read the chart. One line per agency: the higher it is, the better the rating. We keep the lowest of the three. Moody’s writes its ratings differently: Aa3 equals AA-.

              Who measures it, and when. S&P, Moody’s and Fitch. EU rules require them to publish their review dates for the coming year every late December, always on a Friday, and to announce a rating only after markets close (credit rating agencies regulation, Article 8a); they may depart from this calendar only with a justification. Ratings listed by the Agence France Trésor. Collected every morning, a change is recorded only if two independent sources confirm it (Wikipedia, countryeconomy.com).

                  Banque de France TARGET2 balance

                  -3.1%Stress
                  Show the descriptionHide the description

                  What it is. The balance of the Banque de France with the other central banks of the euro area, relative to the size of their combined balance sheet, that of the Eurosystem.

                  Why it matters. When savers, companies or investors move their money from France to a bank in another euro area country, the balance falls. A sharp drop signals capital flight: -6.0% in November 2008, -4.2% in January 2012, at the height of the euro crisis. We divide it by the Eurosystem balance sheet because ECB debt purchases, which have tripled that balance sheet since 2008, also deepen the balance without any panic: in billions, October 2025 (-240) looked worse than 2008 (-119); relative to the balance sheet (-3.9%), it is not.

                  How to read the chart. The line shows the balance at the end of each month, as a % of the Eurosystem balance sheet; the amount in billions appears on hover. The lower it goes, the more capital is leaving France. Coloured backgrounds show the risk thresholds: below -4%, only 2008-2009 and the euro crisis.

                  Who measures it, and when. The European Central Bank, which publishes the Banque de France balance at the end of each month and the Eurosystem balance sheet every week, since 2008; read directly from it every morning.
